How much data does a study need?

Before we collect or buy spatial data, we want to know how many patients to include, how many cells to measure in each, and what size of effect we can hope to find. This article answers these questions with a benchmark on simulated tissues, in which we plant effects of known size and count how many spiDE finds, and with checks on real cohorts at small sample sizes. We also explain the defaults that spiDE uses for small studies of 5 to 7 patients per condition, and which false discoveries to expect.

Together with vignette("spiDE-calibration"), this is one of the two places in the package that quote benchmark numbers. That article shows that the tests are calibrated and accurate, and this one shows what they can find. The workflow is in vignette("spiDE") and the statistics are in vignette("spiDE-model").

The benchmark

The benchmark measured how recall and false discoveries change with the design. It used four simulated tissues, built from the real cell positions, cell types, patients and niche maps of four cohorts, and 960 simulated datasets. Each was tested with the slopes engine of spiDE 0.99.37 at its defaults. We varied three things:

  • the number of patients, 6 to 32, half per condition;
  • the number of cells per patient and index type, 25 to 1,000;
  • the gene panel.

Every dataset carried planted niche effects of six sizes on known genes, so we could count how many of them were found. To say how big an effect is, we use a standardised slope, which is a log fold change per within-patient standard deviation of the log1p niche density. A standardised slope of 0.2 is about a 1.6-fold change between index cells at the niche’s 10th and 90th percentiles, and 0.4 is about 2.7-fold. For comparison, on the three public cohorts the median pooled call is 0.07 to 0.14.

To read the table for a planned study, we find the row with the number of patients and the column of the tissue that looks most like ours. Effects smaller than the entry are found less than 80% of the time, and larger effects are found more often. An entry of “> 0.8” means that none of the sizes tested, up to 0.8, reached 80% recall. We then compare the entry with the effect we expect. The median pooled call on the three public cohorts is a standardised slope of 0.07 to 0.14, and that is below every entry of the table at 12 or fewer patients. The tissues also differ widely within a row, so the table is best used as a range and not as a promise for a tissue that was not simulated.

Patients and cells per patient

The most important lesson of the benchmark is that patients set the power. For a fixed number of cells, dividing them among more patients did better in all but 5 of 528 comparisons (spiDE 0.99.33), none of them beyond noise. At 3,200 cells per type on GSE289194, 8 patients found 2% of effects of 0.4, 16 patients 50% and 32 patients 84%. With 3 patients per condition, the condition-specific test passes its guard (a Kish effective number of at least 3 patients per condition) only for genes whose patient weights are equal, so in practice it does not run.

Cells help once the patients are adequate. At 32 patients, recall of effects of 0.2 on GSE289194 rose from 0.64 at 200 cells to 0.85 at 1,000. Recall also rises steadily with the gene’s expression in the index type, so dim genes are found last.

The pooled test’s degrees of freedom matter most with few patients. spiDE 0.99.37 refers the pooled test to limma’s degrees of freedom scaled by the effective share of the patients (the proportional rule). The rule used before spiDE 0.99.37 capped them at the effective number of patients less one, which made the test conservative: its null spread was 0.83 to 0.88 at 6 patients and 0.91 to 0.97 at 12. It is now 0.94 to 1.05 at every size.

The condition-specific test

The condition-specific test needs more data than the pooled test, because it compares two groups of patients and has fewer patients in each. Where the condition test and the pooled test both reach 80% recall, the condition test needs 2.0 to 2.3 times the pooled test’s effect, for a slope present in one condition only (median by tissue).

The next table gives the recall at 200 cells, averaged over effect sizes and the four tissues, for a slope present in one condition only (“one condition”) and one that reverses between them (“reversing”). All three families use the pooled test of 0.99.37:

patients default family: one condition / reversing pooled filter only ("filtered"): one condition / reversing all triplets: one condition / reversing
12 0.26 / 0.25 0.03 / 0.00 0.15 / 0.17
16 0.32 / 0.29 0.08 / 0.00 0.22 / 0.25
24 0.39 / 0.34 0.24 / 0.01 0.33 / 0.33
32 0.42 / 0.37 0.33 / 0.00 0.39 / 0.39

The default family, procedure = "pooled_or_heterogeneity", adds the triplets whose slopes vary between patients beyond their sampling noise to those that pass the pooled test. It finds reversing effects, which the pooled filter alone cannot. With few patients it also finds more than testing every triplet does, because its smaller family carries a lighter multiple-testing correction.

Small designs: 5 to 7 patients per condition

Many studies have only 5 to 7 patients per condition. Two choices of spiDE 0.99.37 were made for them. One is the pooled test’s degrees of freedom (the proportional rule), and the other is the condition test’s family (procedure = "pooled_or_heterogeneity"). Each was confirmed on null data that no earlier step had used, against criteria written before those data were scored.

Neither choice was straightforward, so we say how each came about. The degrees of freedom were not the first choice of their study. The study’s pre-set rule rejected them because the pooled false discovery proportion rose on the simulated benchmark (see “False discoveries to expect”). They were adopted after a follow-up, whose criteria they passed. The condition family is the second version. The heterogeneity filter alone was confirmed first. The pooled filter was added to it after the heterogeneity filter alone missed a modest difference on a strong pooled slope in the toy data, and the combination was then confirmed on fresh nulls.

We checked both on the real cohorts in two ways. The first was to use fewer patients. Patients were drawn at random from each cohort, 6 to 16 at a time with half per condition, on block-null maps the studies had not used. The pooled test’s null spread was 0.88 to 0.98 with the new degrees of freedom, against 0.76 to 0.95 with the old cap, and its block tail was at most 0.015. On the same subsampled real data, the new degrees of freedom raised the pooled test’s recall of effects of one between-patient standard deviation: at 16 patients, from 0.07 to 0.37 on GSE282639 and from 0.07 to 0.35 on GSE289194. Its false discovery proportion stayed below 0.035.

The second was to add known effects to real slopes. Condition effects of known size were added to the real patients’ slopes after their condition labels were shuffled, so everything else (the patients, their noise and their imbalance) is real. The effects were either present in one condition only or reversing between the conditions, at four sizes. The table gives the recall at 12 / 16 patients, the mean over both kinds and the four sizes, for 0.99.37’s default family with its pooled test:

cohort default family pooled filter of 0.99.36
GSE250346 0.36 / 0.56 0.00 / 0.00
GSE282639 0.59 / 0.69 0.00 / 0.00
GSE289194 0.22 / 0.42 0.00 / 0.00

The false discovery proportion was 0.004 to 0.028. With every patient, the default family found 0.49 of the effects on GSE282639 and 0.55 on GSE289194, against 0.05 and 0.13 with the pooled filter of 0.99.36. On GSE250346 with every patient (35, 9 of them controls) the result was mixed. The default family found fewer of the effects present in one condition only, 0.23 against 0.55, and more of the reversing ones, 0.24 against 0.01. On that cohort the pooled filter already passes almost half of the triplets, so the larger family likely costs more in the multiple-testing correction than it adds.

False discoveries to expect

Power is only useful if the calls can be trusted, so the benchmark also counted false discoveries. For the condition-specific test the news is good. No all-null dataset made a condition-specific call in the default family at BH 0.05. Averaged over the tissues, 2.8% to 4.1% of the condition permutations made one at each size, against a limit of 7%, and single tissues reached 6%. The condition test’s false discovery proportion averaged at most 0.06 at any size on GSE289194, GSE282639 and YTMA. On GSE250346 it reached 0.14 at 32 patients, above nominal.

For the pooled test the false discovery proportion ran above nominal in the most precise designs. On GSE289194, GSE282639 and YTMA it averaged 0.04 up to 16 patients, 0.06 at 24 and 0.08 at 32 (0.99.36: up to 0.04), with single designs up to 0.20. On the 343-gene panel of GSE250346 it averaged 0.09 to 0.19 (0.99.36: up to 0.15).

The measurements point to a compositional cause. Strong niche effects in bright genes move the library size with the niche, and relative to the library every other gene in that (index, niche) column then moves the other way. Those changes are real changes in a gene’s share of the counts, but they are not niche effects on the gene itself. The rise with 0.99.37 is attributed to these changes, which the larger degrees of freedom detect along with real effects. This has not been isolated. The pooled test’s spread on all-null datasets is 0.94 to 1.05, and the real-data checks above show no rise.

Practical recommendations

We can draw some advice from these numbers.

  • Spend on patients first. For a fixed number of cells, more patients did better, and cells helped only once the patients were adequate. Where the budget allows a choice, add patients before adding cells.
  • Plan the effect size. Compare the effect that matters with the minimum detectable effect of the table above. At 12 or fewer patients, effects as small as the median pooled call of the public cohorts (0.07 to 0.14) are below every entry.
  • Plan more for the condition-specific test. It needs 2.0 to 2.3 times the pooled test’s effect, so a study sized for the pooled test is underpowered for a difference between conditions.
  • With 3 patients per condition, expect no condition-specific test. Each condition needs a Kish effective number of at least 3 patients, which 3 patients reach only with equal weights.
  • Keep the default family for small studies. For 5 to 7 patients per condition the default, procedure = "pooled_or_heterogeneity", found the most and, unlike the pooled filter alone, finds reversing effects.
  • Check columns with many weak calls. If many weak pooled calls share one (index, niche) column and one sign, check that column’s median slope and the library size against the niche. That is the signature of the compositional effect described above.

The full results of spiDE 0.99.33 and the design of the simulations are described in (Bhuva et al., in preparation).
