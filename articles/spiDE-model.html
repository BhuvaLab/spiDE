In this article we explain what spiDE computes and why, one step at a time. We start each step in plain language and then give the formula, followed by what the formula says in words, so the text can be read with or without the maths. It holds all the statistics of the package, from the model of a single gene to the tests of gene sets and of niche abundance. How to run the functions is in vignette("spiDE"), how well the tests hold up on real and simulated data is in vignette("spiDE-calibration"), and how many patients and cells a study needs is in vignette("spiDE-design").

The question

Take one cell type, the index type (say, tumour cells), and a second cell type, the niche type (say, fibroblasts). spiDE asks whether a gene’s expression in the index cells changes with the number of niche cells around them.

  • The pooled test asks: across patients, is there on average such a change?
  • The condition-specific test asks: does the change differ between two groups of patients (responders and non-responders, early and late stage)?

One gene, one index type and one niche type make a triplet. spiDE tests every triplet.

Both questions are about patients, not cells. The condition belongs to a patient, and so does the way a patient’s tissue responds to its neighbourhood. spiDE therefore treats the patient as the unit of replication. A result is strong when patients agree, not when a patient contributes many cells. Treating cells as replicates would make every result look more certain the more cells were measured, even if patients disagreed.

What kinds of effects can spiDE find?

A gene’s expression in the index cells can be tied to a niche type in more than one way, and a condition can change each of them. Each of spiDE’s tests is built for one kind of effect, and two common effects of a condition are left to other tools. The first thing to separate is the scale of the comparison:

  • Within a patient. In the same tissue, do index cells with more niche cells around them express more (or less) of the gene than index cells with fewer? This compares cells that share everything that belongs to the patient: its genetics, its treatment, how its sample was handled, and how much of each cell type its tissue contains.
  • Between patients. Do patients whose index cells sit, on average, among more of the niche type express more (or less) of the gene overall? This compares patients with one another, one value per patient.

Each of the two can be the same in both groups of patients or differ between them. The figure below draws every case on mock data, together with two effects of a condition that spiDE does not test.

Within patients: what testSpiDE() tests

  • A. The same slope in every patient (independent of the condition). Within each patient, the gene rises (or falls) with the density of the niche type, and the patients agree. Patients may differ in their overall level of the gene, as the lines in panel A sit at different heights; that does not matter, because only the slopes are compared. The pooled test detects it (testSpiDE(), then results(res, test = "pooled")). A call means: in the same tissue, index cells with more of this niche type around them express more (or less) of the gene, consistently across patients.
  • B. A slope that differs between the groups (condition-specific). The slope is present in one group only (panel B), is stronger in one group, or runs in opposite directions. The condition-specific test detects it (testSpiDE(condition = ), then results(res, test = "condition")), as the difference in average slope, second level minus first. A call means: the index cells’ response to each extra unit of niche density differs between the groups.

By default the condition-specific test is run only among triplets that pass one of two filters (see “Multiple testing”): the pooled test, or a test that the slopes vary between patients more than their sampling noise allows. A slope present in one group only, or stronger in one group, has a non-zero average and can pass the first. All three patterns make the slopes vary between patients and can pass the second, including a slope that reverses between the groups and averages out close to zero. To spiDE 0.99.36 only the first filter was used (procedure = "filtered"), so a reversing slope was unlikely to reach the condition-specific test.

A within-patient slope is not always a response of the index cells. Transcripts of neighbouring niche cells can be assigned to index cells when cells are segmented, and they too rise with the niche’s density. plotSpillover() (see vignette("spiDE")) shows whether the calls are the niche type’s own markers.

Between patients: what testNicheAbundance() tests

spiDE’s model gives each patient its own baseline level of every gene (the patient intercepts of Step 2). Everything that differs between patients as a whole is set aside before the slopes are estimated, including a real association: patients whose index cells sit among more of the niche type may express the gene differently overall. testNicheAbundance() asks that question separately, on one pseudobulk profile per patient and index type, against the patient’s mean niche density.

  • C. Patients with more of the niche differ (independent of the condition). Within each patient the gene is flat, but the patients’ means rise with their mean niche density (panel C). The term "niche" of testNicheAbundance() detects it: across all patients without a condition, averaged over the two groups with one. A call means: patients whose index cells sit among more of this niche type have more (or less) of the gene in those cells.
  • D. That association differs between the groups (condition-specific). The patients’ means rise with their niche density in one group only (panel D). The term "condition:niche" of testNicheAbundance(condition = ) detects it, as the difference between the groups, second level minus first.

These are statements about patients, not about cells responding to their neighbours. Patients with more of a niche type differ in many other ways too: in their overall composition, in sample quality, or in signal spilling between cells across the whole section. A between-patient association cannot be pinned on the niche, which is why spiDE’s own tests work within patients. Report each result as what it is, and never a result of one test as a result of the other.

What spiDE does not test

Two effects of a condition are absorbed by the patients’ own levels, and no spiDE test looks at them:

  • E. The gene’s level differs between the groups, whatever the neighbourhood (panel E). This is ordinary differential expression between conditions. Test it on pseudobulk profiles with a differential expression method such as limma or edgeR.
  • F. The amount of the niche differs between the groups (panel F): a change in tissue composition, here more of the niche type around the index cells of late-stage patients, while expression does not change with it. Test it with a composition method, such as sccomp or speckle for cell-type proportions.

This matters most for a condition such as disease progression, which changes tissue in many ways at once. Most of those changes are differences between patients: late-stage tissue has more of some cell types and less of others (F), and its cells express some genes more or less (E), often both together and often in cell types that interact. Neither is a change in how cells respond to their neighbours, and the condition-specific test sees neither. It sees only a changed response per unit of neighbour density (B): within late-stage tissue, index cells with more niche cells around them differ from index cells with fewer by more, by less or in the opposite direction than within early-stage tissue. A changed interaction between two cell types reaches this test only when it changes that response. “Late-stage tumours have more fibroblasts, and their tumour cells express more of a gene” is F and E, not B, even when both happen in the same patients.

The effects in the model

Reduced to one niche type, with each patient’s niche densities centred at their mean, the model of Step 2 reads, for gene \(g\) in index cell \(i\) of patient \(s\):

\[ \log \mathrm{E}[y_{gi}] \;=\; a_{gs} \;+\; \beta_{gs}\,(\ell_i - \bar\ell_s), \]

where \(\ell_i\) is the cell’s niche density on the log1p scale, \(\bar\ell_s\) the mean of \(\ell_i\) over the patient’s index cells, \(\beta_{gs}\) the patient’s within-patient slope and \(a_{gs}\) its level at its own mean density. With \(c_s\) the patient’s group, each effect is a statement about how these quantities vary across patients:

A. slope in every patient the average \(\beta_{gs}\) is not 0 testSpiDE(), pooled test within a patient, index cells with more of the niche around them express more (or less) of the gene
B. slope differs between groups the average \(\beta_{gs}\) differs with \(c_s\) testSpiDE(condition = ), condition-specific test that within-patient response differs between the groups
C. patients with more niche differ \(a_{gs}\) changes with \(\bar\ell_s\) testNicheAbundance(), term "niche" patients whose index cells sit among more of the niche differ in the gene’s level
D. that association differs between groups the change of \(a_{gs}\) with \(\bar\ell_s\) differs with \(c_s\) testNicheAbundance(condition = ), term "condition:niche" the between-patient association differs between the groups
E. level differs between groups \(a_{gs}\) differs with \(c_s\) not spiDE: pseudobulk differential expression the gene’s overall level differs between the groups

spiDE’s tests read only the \(\beta_{gs}\). The intercepts \(a_{gs}\) absorb C to E, and F changes only where along the density axis each patient’s slope is measured.

Gene sets can be tested for the first four effects: A and B with spiGSEA(), C and D with testNicheAbundance(genesets = ).

Step 1: the niche

For every cell, buildNiches() measures how many cells of each type surround it. It places a Gaussian bump of width \(\sigma\) (the bandwidth, in the units of the spatial coordinates) on every cell of a type and adds them up, which gives a smooth density of that type at every point of the tissue. The density of type \(n\) around cell \(i\) is \(d_{in}\).

spiDE uses \(\ell_{in} = \log(1 + d_{in})\), which compresses very dense neighbourhoods so that a few crowded areas do not dominate.

The bandwidth sets what “nearby” means: a small \(\sigma\) looks at immediate neighbours, a larger one at the surrounding tissue. Fit one bandwidth at a time.

Step 2: a regression within each index type

Each index type is analysed separately. Within it, each gene’s counts are modelled by a negative binomial regression, the standard model for sequencing counts. Its mean can depend on covariates and its variance allows for more spread than pure sampling noise. For gene \(g\) in cell \(i\) of patient \(s(i)\):

\[ \log \mathrm{E}[y_{gi}] \;=\; \underbrace{a_{g,s(i)}}_{\text{patient's own level}} \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma_g^\top z_i}_{\text{cell covariates}} \;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{n} \beta_{gn}\, \ell_{in}}_{\text{niche slopes}} \]

  • \(a_{gs}\), one intercept per patient. Each patient gets its own baseline level of the gene. This absorbs everything that is constant within a patient: its average expression, its sample handling, its condition, and its average niche. What is left to explain the niche slopes is variation within each patient: cells of the same patient in denser or sparser neighbourhoods. So \(\beta_{gn}\) is a within-patient slope. It answers “in the same tissue, do cells with more neighbours of type \(n\) express more of the gene?”, not “do patients with more type-\(n\) cells express more of it?”.
  • \(z_i\), cell covariates. By default the cell’s log library size (its total count, centred), with a slope per gene. This adjusts for sequencing depth (depth = "loglib"; see the options below). Other cell-level measurements, such as cell area, are added with covariates.
  • \(\ell_{in}\), the niche columns. Every cell type other than the index type enters, so each slope is adjusted for the other niches. Only the types named in niche are tested. An index type is never tested against its own niche, including any mergeNiches() group it belongs to.
  • The dispersion \(\psi_g\) measures how much more variable the gene is than pure counting noise. It is estimated per gene.

The fit uses SpaNorm::polishNB(), which fits each gene on its own. The patient intercepts are handled algebraically, so a fit with many patients costs about the same as one with few. Genes are independent given an index type, so they can be fitted in parallel.

Depth options. depth = "loglib" (the default) uses one slope on the log library size. "nonlinear" lets the effect of depth bend (a spline with three degrees of freedom). "spatial_spline" lets it vary smoothly across each section. "none" leaves depth out, for use with offset, a precomputed log-scale offset per gene and cell. Library size mixes technical capture with biological RNA content, so adjusting for it also removes any part of the niche effect that works through a cell’s total RNA. A slope is then expression relative to the cell’s total.

Step 3: each patient’s own slope

The regression above gives one pooled slope per gene and niche. The slopes engine, the default, then asks what each patient’s own slope is.

Refitting the model for every patient would be slow and unstable for patients with few cells. Instead, spiDE starts from the pooled fit and lets each patient’s own cells move the slope once toward what fits that patient best. This is a single step of the Newton-type algorithm (Fisher scoring) used to fit such models. With working weights \(w_i = \mu_i/(1 + \psi\mu_i)\) and residuals \(r_i = (y_i - \mu_i)/(1 + \psi\mu_i)\) at the pooled fit, and \(\tilde\ell_i\) the niche columns centred within the patient:

\[ \hat\beta_{gs} = \hat\beta_g + I_s^{-1} u_s, \qquad I_s = \sum_{i \in s} w_i \tilde\ell_i \tilde\ell_i^\top, \qquad u_s = \sum_{i \in s} \tilde\ell_i\, r_i . \]

\(u_s\) is how strongly the patient’s cells pull away from the pooled slope, and \(I_s\) is how much information they carry. A patient with many cells and a wide range of niche densities has a large \(I_s\) and a precise slope. A patient whose niche columns barely vary, or vary together, gives no usable slope for that gene and is left out of its test.

How precise is a patient’s slope? Two answers are computed.

  • The model variance, \(v^{\text{model}}_{gs} = \mathrm{diag}(I_s^{-1})\), is what the regression would give if every cell were independent.
  • Neighbouring cells are not independent: they share local tissue conditions, so their residual expression is similar. A spatial variance \(v^{\text{tile}}\) corrects for this. Each section is cut into square tiles (side tile, default \(3\sigma\)), the patient’s score is summed per tile, and the slope’s variance is computed from how much the tile sums vary: \(v^{\text{tile}} = \mathrm{diag}\big(I_s^{-1} (\sum_t U_t U_t^\top) I_s^{-1}\big)\, T/(T-1)\) over the \(T\) tiles, with \(U_t\) the score of tile \(t\). A patient with fewer than min.tiles tiles takes the model variance scaled by the typical ratio.

The tile variance of a single gene is noisy, so spiDE summarises it as one inflation factor per patient and niche, shared by all genes: \(f_{sn} = \max\{1, \mathrm{median}_g(v^{\text{tile}}_{gsn}/v^{\text{model}}_{gsn})\}\). A patient’s slope variance is then \(v_{gs} = v^{\text{model}}_{gs} f_{sn}\).

Because the fit never sees the condition, any patient-level condition can be tested afterwards (testSpiDE(fit, condition = ...)). Retesting under a reshuffled condition reruns only the test, which makes permutation checks cheap.

Step 4: combining patients

Each gene now has one slope per patient, each with a variance. The tests combine them across patients.

How much patients disagree. Patients’ slopes differ for two reasons: each is estimated with noise (\(v_{gs}\)), and patients genuinely differ. The genuine spread is the between-patient variance \(\tau^2_g\), estimated from how much more the slopes scatter than their noise explains (the DerSimonian-Laird estimator of meta-analysis, computed without the condition).

Weights. Patient \(s\) is weighted by

\[ w_{gs} = \frac{1}{v_{gs} + \tau^2_g}. \]

A precise patient counts more, but never unboundedly more. When patients genuinely disagree (\(\tau^2_g\) large), every patient’s weight approaches the same value, because no single patient can speak for the rest.

The pooled test

Is the average slope zero? The weighted average of the patients’ slopes is tested with limma’s moderated \(t\)-test. The moderated test borrows information across genes to stabilise each gene’s variance, which helps when patients are few. Two refinements keep it honest:

  • Borrowing is matched by expression level. A bright gene’s variability is compared with other bright genes, not with the typical gene (eBayes(trend = TRUE, robust = TRUE) on the gene’s log mean expression).
  • The degrees of freedom count effective patients. When a few cell-rich patients carry most of the weight, the test should not behave as if every patient contributed equally. The Kish effective number of patients, \(n_{\text{eff}} = (\sum_s w_s)^2 / \sum_s w_s^2\), equals the number of patients \(m\) when the weights are equal and falls towards one as they concentrate. The \(t\) statistic is referred to \(\nu_{\text{limma}} \, n_{\text{eff}} / m\) degrees of freedom (the proportional rule): limma’s degrees of freedom, which include those borrowed from the other genes, scaled by the effective share of the patients. To spiDE 0.99.36 they were \(\min(\nu_{\text{limma}}, n_{\text{eff}} - 1)\) (the capped rule). Since \(n_{\text{eff}} \le m\), that cap always applied and discarded the borrowed degrees of freedom, which made the test conservative with few patients.

The condition-specific test

Does the average slope differ between the two groups? The patients’ slopes are regressed on the condition, with the same weights \(w_{gs}\):

\[ \hat\beta_{gs} = b_0 + \Delta_g\, c_s + [\text{strata}] + \text{error}, \]

where \(c_s\) is 1 for the second condition and 0 for the first. The estimate \(\hat\Delta_g\) is the difference in average slope, second level minus first.

Its standard error does not assume the weights are exactly right. It is a robust (HC2 sandwich) standard error, computed from how far each patient’s slope actually sits from its group’s fit, with small-sample Bell-McCaffrey degrees of freedom. This matters when some patients’ slopes are noisier than their weights suggest. A marker gene that is patchy within the index type is an example: its slope can swing with where the patches fall. Such a patient widens the interval rather than producing a call.

The weights come from the gene-shared variances \(v_{gs}\) and never from a gene’s own tile variance. A gene’s own tile variance, computed at the pooled fit, grows when a patient’s slope sits far from the pooled slope. Weighting by it would down-weight exactly the patients that differ most and shrink the estimated difference toward zero.

strata (a patient-level slide or batch column) adds one term per stratum to the condition model. Use it when the condition is partly confounded with slide, so that slide differences in slope are not read as condition differences. A condition entirely confounded with the strata cannot be tested and is refused.

Multiple testing

spiDE tests many triplets, so it controls the false discovery rate (the expected share of false calls among the calls) with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. testSpiDE() reports a \(q\)-value per row:

  • pooled: over every tested triplet;
  • condition-specific: over a family of triplets chosen without looking at the condition, so that the family is the same under any relabelling of patients and the false discovery rate is preserved. Rows outside the family have q = NA.
    • procedure = "pooled_or_heterogeneity" (the default for the slopes engine): the triplets that pass either of the next two filters.
    • procedure = "heterogeneity": the triplets whose slopes vary between patients more than their sampling variances allow. The statistic is Cochran’s \(Q_g = \sum_s (\hat\beta_{gs} - \bar\beta_g)^2 / v_{gs}\), the same one behind \(\tau^2_g\), referred to \(\chi^2_{m-1}\). Its \(p\)-value is reported as p.heterogeneity and filtered by Benjamini-Hochberg over every triplet at fdr. A difference between the conditions is such variation, so the correction is spent where there is something for the condition to explain, including a slope that reverses between the groups. With few patients this filter carries the condition test.
    • procedure = "filtered" (the default to spiDE 0.99.36, and the sandwich engine’s): the triplets whose pooled test passes at fdr. With few patients the pooled test passes few triplets, and a reversing slope rarely passes it. It keeps a modest difference on a strong pooled slope, which \(Q_g\), spread over \(m - 1\) degrees of freedom, can miss.
    • procedure = "all": every triplet.

The estimate is on the natural-log scale per unit \(\log(1 + d)\). A pooled slope of 0.2 means expression rises by about 22% (\(e^{0.2}\)) for each unit increase in \(\log(1 + \text{density})\), in the same patient.

The sandwich engine

engine = "sandwich" takes a different route to the same question. It fits the regression of Step 2 once, with the condition’s interaction terms \(c_s \ell_{in}\) when given a condition, and tests the coefficients directly. Its standard error is a patient-clustered CR2 sandwich (Bell and McCaffrey 2002; Pustejovsky and Tipton 2018) with Bell-McCaffrey degrees of freedom: it lets patients differ without modelling how. It needs the condition at fit time. It is simpler but less powerful when effects vary between patients, and spiGSEA() needs the slopes engine.

The gene-set test

A gene set is tested exactly like a gene, with spiGSEA(). In each patient, the set’s slope is the average of its genes’ slopes, each gene first divided by its typical spread between patients, \(\sqrt{\tau^2_g + \mathrm{median}_s v_{gs}}\), so that a highly expressed gene does not dominate.

  • Competitive (the default): the same average over the other tested genes is subtracted. The test then asks whether the set moves more than the typical gene. This guards against a shift that moves every gene at once, such as signal spilling over from neighbouring cells.
  • Self-contained: nothing is subtracted, and the test asks whether the set moves at all.

The patients’ set slopes go through the same pooled and condition-specific tests as genes’ slopes. Because patients are the replicates, the correlation between genes of a set is already reflected in how much the set’s slope varies from patient to patient, and needs no separate estimate. \(q\)-values are given within each (index, niche, test) column and over every set tested (q.global).

The niche-abundance test

The patient intercepts of Step 2 absorb one association on purpose. Patients whose index cells sit, on average, among more niche cells may express a gene differently as a whole. That is a comparison between patients, with as many data points as patients. It can reflect biology, differences in tissue composition, or signal spilling between neighbouring cells. testNicheAbundance() asks it directly, in two steps:

  • per patient and index type, it averages expression into a pseudobulk profile (log counts per million) and computes the patients’ mean niche density;
  • it regresses one on the other across patients with limma, adding the difference between conditions when a condition is given.

Without a condition, the term "niche" is the association across all patients (effect C of What kinds of effects can spiDE find?). With a condition, "niche" is the association averaged over the two conditions, and the term "condition:niche" is its difference between them, second level minus first (effect D). The two terms are separate multiple-testing families.

With a few dozen patients, one patient at the edge of a niche type’s abundance can carry a whole between-patient slope; if a gene is also unusual in that patient, the slope looks far more certain than it is. So patients whose leverage in the regression exceeds max.leverage times the average are down-weighted until none does. The weights come from the patients’ niche densities and conditions only, never from expression, so no gene’s own values decide how much a patient counts. Each row reports the largest leverage before this cap and how many patients were down-weighted.

A result from this test says “patients with more of this niche differ”. A result from testSpiDE() says “within a patient, cells with more of this niche differ”. Report each as what it is.

With genesets, testNicheAbundance() tests gene sets the same way. Each gene is standardised across patients, and a set’s score in a patient is the mean of its genes’ standardised pseudobulk expression. With type = "competitive" (the default), the mean over the other genes is subtracted, as in spiGSEA(). The scores then go through the same regression as genes. This set version has not yet been checked against permutation nulls.

What the model does not address

  • Smoothness of the niche itself. A niche density is smooth over space. The spatial variance accounts for residual expression that is similar in neighbouring cells, and the calibration checks use nulls that keep the niche’s smoothness, but the model does not describe it.
  • Straight-line effects. Each slope is linear in \(\log(1 + d)\). A gene that responds only above a threshold density is captured only partly.
  • Which average. The pooled slope is a weighted average over patients, with weights that favour precise patients. When cell-rich patients have systematically different slopes from sparse ones, this average differs from a simple average over patients.
